How to describe pain to your doctor

By Chris Greenfield, founder of WellNest · Published 23 September 2026

Pain is invisible, and appointments are short. These six things – plus a list of words that actually describe how pain feels – help your GP, specialist or physio understand what you're living with.

1. Where it is

Point to the exact spot and say whether it stays there or spreads. "Right foot, spreading up to the ankle" is far more useful than "my leg hurts". If it moves around, say so.

2. How strong it is – and what the number means to you

Most clinicians use a 0–10 scale, where 0 is no pain and 10 is the worst pain you can imagine (see our printable pain scale chart). Numbers mean different things to different people, so add what it stops you doing: "6 out of 10 – I can work, but I can't walk to the shops". Give your usual level, your worst level and your best level over the past few weeks, not just how you feel in the waiting room.

3. What it feels like

The type of pain can point towards its cause, so the words you choose matter. Pick the ones that fit:

WordWhat it usually means
Achinga dull, steady soreness
Burningheat or a hot, raw feeling
Stabbingsudden, sharp, like being jabbed
Shootingpain that travels quickly along a line
Throbbingpulsing, often in time with your heartbeat
Electriclike a jolt or electric shock
Tingling / pins and needlesprickling or buzzing
Crampingsqueezing or tightening
Stiffhard to move, especially after rest
Sensitive to touchlight touch, clothing or bedsheets hurt

4. When it happens and how long it lasts

Is it constant, or does it come and go? How long does a flare last – minutes, hours, days? Is it worse in the morning, at night or after activity? Patterns like these often matter more to a doctor than any single description.

5. What makes it better or worse

Mention possible triggers – activity, sitting, weather, stress, poor sleep – and what you've tried: medication, heat or cold, rest, stretches, physio exercises. Say what helped, what didn't, and for how long.

6. How it affects your life

This is the part people most often leave out, and it's usually the most important. Can you sleep, work, drive, look after children, shower or cook? "I've stopped going to work two days a week" tells a doctor more than "it's really bad".

Put it together

Example: "For the last six weeks I've had burning pain in my right foot that spreads to the ankle. It's usually 5 out of 10, up to 8 after walking, and it wakes me most nights. Light touch from socks hurts. Rest and keeping it cool help a little; paracetamol doesn't. I've had to cut my work hours."

Bring a record, not just your memory

Pain is hard to recall accurately weeks later, especially on a good day in the consulting room. A few weeks of diary entries gives your doctor real patterns to work with. FAQ

How do I describe nerve pain?

People often describe nerve pain as burning, shooting, electric, tingling or pins and needles, sometimes with numbness or extreme sensitivity to light touch. Tell your doctor which of these fit and where you feel them.

What does 7 out of 10 pain mean?

On the common 0–10 scale, 7 is usually severe pain that stops you doing normal activities and is hard to ignore. Because people use the scale differently, explain what your number stops you from doing.

Should I play down my pain at the doctor?

No. Describe your typical and worst days honestly, including how pain affects sleep, work and daily tasks. Doctors rely on your account to decide on tests and treatment.

How can I remember everything in a short appointment?

Keep a symptom diary for two to four weeks beforehand and bring a short written summary with your top two or three questions.
